The part that makes it work

The teacher is assumed to know nothing

Not "a little rusty". Nothing. No AI, no Python, and from year three, no calculus either. That assumption shaped every page.

Every single week, the adult gets:

  • A section that teaches you the topic first — before it asks you to teach it
  • A minute-by-minute script, with sentences you can read aloud
  • The exact questions to ask — and what to do when the answer isn't what you hoped
  • The errors the class will hit, with the real message and the fix
  • A complete answer key

Preparation is 15–20 minutes the night before. That's the design target, and the guide tells you exactly what to read.

There is also a one-off orientation — a plain-English crash course in AI for the adult. Roughly an hour, once, before week one.

This is the slide that decides the room. If a parent believes this, they'll try it. Offer to show them a real week's teacher file afterwards.

The subject itself

So — how does an AI model actually work?

Here is the whole idea, in the form your child meets it in week two. You can explain this at dinner tonight.

Old way: a person writes the rule.  “If the message contains free money, call it spam.”
Machine learning: nobody writes the rule. You show the machine thousands of messages already labelled spam or not spam, and it works the rule out itself.

If you only land one thing: nobody wrote the rule. That single sentence is the whole shift, and it's what makes AI feel like magic until you see it.

The subject itself

And that's exactly why it gets things wrong

A model only ever knows the examples it was shown. Your child learns to ask which examples? before trusting any answer — the most transferable habit in the course.

📸

Shown only sunny photos

…it fails in the rain. It never learned what rain looks like, so it guesses confidently and wrongly.

⚖️

Shown mostly one kind of person

…it works worse for everyone else. This is what "bias" means in practice — not malice, just a lopsided pile of examples.

🎯

Tested on what it studied

…the score is a lie. So we hide some examples and test on those. Your child does this in year one, with index cards.

The habit this builds: "How do you know?" and "Who's missing from the data?" Those questions outlast any particular technology.

The sunny-photos example is the keeper. Parents remember it and repeat it, which is how the idea gets into the household.

The three years

What changes, year by year

YearAgeWhat they doWhat they can say at the end
1 · Explorer~11 No code at all. Card games, spreadsheets, and free browser tools that train a model from your webcam. "I trained a model, then I broke it on purpose, and I can tell you why it failed."
2 · Builder~12–13 Their first programming, from print("hello") to real data tables and their first genuine models. "I wrote the program, I cleaned the data, and I can show you the chart that answers my question."
3 · Engineer~14–15 How learning works underneath — then building a small neural network by hand, and one that reads handwritten digits. "I built a brain out of arithmetic, and I can prove my score is honest."

Each year is a full school year and ends with a showcase: something built, presented, and defended out loud.

Read the right-hand column aloud. Those are the sentences a parent wants to hear their kid say — that's the product.

Why this order

Nothing arrives before it's needed

The commonest way a young person is lost to this subject is being shown maths or syntax they had no preparation for. So the course counts what it introduces, week by week.

At most 4 new pieces of code a week. Every command is logged against the week it first appears, and it is never used earlier.

At most one new mathematical idea a week — and it is always counted on paper first, named second.

What that looks like in practice

Year 3 needs the idea of a slope — that's calculus territory. So weeks earlier, your child measures steepness the way you'd measure a hill: how far up, divided by how far along, with actual numbers, on squared paper.

By the time the word gradient appears, they've already done the thing it names. The word is a label for something familiar, not a new mountain.

This is checked mechanically, not just intended — a term used before the week that teaches it is treated as a fault in the course.

For the sceptical parent — often one who found maths cold at school. This is the slide that says "it won't happen to your kid the way it happened to you."

The unusual bit

They're marked on telling the truth

Most courses reward the demo that works. This one repeatedly asks your child to try to break their own work and report what they find.

Written into the assignments

  • Hide some examples; test on those, not on what you studied
  • Report the score that embarrasses you
  • Say who your data left out
  • Ask who gets hurt when this is wrong

In the worked example a student is shown deliberately: their first accuracy was 0.99. It was wrong — the test had leaked into the practice. The honest number was lower. Finding that earns the marks.

The same thread runs from year one — fairness, privacy, and not over-trusting a confident machine — as concrete scenarios to argue about, never as a lecture.

This is the slide that separates it from a coding club. It's also the one teachers tend to like most.

Practicalities

What it costs and what you need

Year 1 — nothing to install

  • A computer with a browser, and a webcam for two or three weeks
  • Index cards, squared paper, coloured pens, poster paper — about the price of a pizza
  • Three free websites, no payment ever, no account needed for two of them
  • 23 of the 36 weeks need no technology at all

Years 2 and 3 — free software

  • Python and its free libraries. One evening of setup, with a step-by-step guide and a troubleshooting table for the dozen things that usually go wrong
  • No internet needed during lessons, and nothing to download mid-class

No subscription. No licence. No cloud account. No GPU. Everything runs on an ordinary laptop.

Privacy: the model-training tool runs inside the browser — pictures needn't be uploaded anywhere. Your child never has to give a site a real name, face or email. The rules for this are written down for the adult before week one.

Your time: about 1 hour 40 minutes a week, including preparation. Plus one hour, once, to read the orientation.

Be concrete about money. "A pizza" is memorable and true for year one.

Honesty

What this is not

🚫

Not a shortcut into a job

It's an education in how a technology works. Nobody here is promising your 14-year-old a salary.

🚫

Not a certificate

There's no accreditation. What they have at the end is work, and the ability to explain it.

🚫

Not self-running

An adult has to show up for an hour a week. The guide does the preparation, not the turning up.

🚫

Not about ChatGPT

Chatbots appear, but the subject is the machinery underneath. Year 4 — the one that builds toward today's large systems — isn't written yet.

Three of the four years are complete. Year 4 is planned but not built. You should know that before you start, not in two years' time.

Say the year-4 gap out loud. Volunteering the limitation is what makes the rest of the deck believable.

A real week

Week 12, year three, as an example

"How steep is the hill right here?"

What happens in the hour

  • They draw a curve and pick one point on it
  • They measure how far up and how far along, over a tiny step — and divide
  • They do it again at a different point and notice the number changes
  • They realise the sign tells them which way is downhill

No symbols. No rules to memorise. A division, on paper, that they did themselves.

That division is how every AI model in the world learns: work out which way is downhill, take a small step, repeat a few thousand times.

Your child meets the mechanism before they meet the vocabulary — and they meet it as arithmetic they can check.

Pick this week deliberately. It's the moment the course either works or loses them, and it shows the method better than any abstract claim.

Questions you might still have

Straight answers

"What if we fall behind?" Nothing breaks. There are no deadlines, and no week depends on a week after it. Restart the same week from the beginning.
"My child is older than 11." Start further in. Each year opens with a check of what you'd need already; if they can do it, skip ahead.
"Can two or three children do it together?" Yes. Every activity carries a note for small groups. Above eight, split into pairs and double the activity time.
"What if I can't answer their question?" Each week lists the questions students actually ask, with honest answers — including ones where the honest answer is "nobody knows for sure."
"Do they need to be good at maths?" They need fractions, percentages and reading a graph. Everything beyond that is built from scratch when it's needed.
Keep a blank slide after this for the question you didn't anticipate. There's always one.
