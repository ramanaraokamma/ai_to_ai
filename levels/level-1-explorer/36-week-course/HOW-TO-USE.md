# 🧭 How To Use This Course

*Read this once. It takes four minutes and it is the only thing you need before Week 1.*

[Course home](README.md) · [Teacher Orientation](teacher-guide/00-orientation.md) · [Week 1 teacher guide](teacher-guide/week-01.md) · [Figure style guide](figures/STYLE.md)

---

> **One sentence:** 36 weeks, one class a week, three books that mirror each other — and you do not
> need to know any AI to teach it, because the teacher guide tells you exactly what to say.

---

## 📚 The three books

Every week exists three times. Same lesson, same numbers, same activity, written for three different
people. **The books are not interchangeable and you should not read them in parallel.**

| Book | Who reads it | Length | What it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[`teacher-guide/`](teacher-guide/)** | You, the adult | ~1,100 lines/week | What to say, minute by minute. Prep list, the misconceptions you will actually meet, differentiation, a full answer key, and a "if something fails" table. Read this **before** class. |
| **[`student-guide/`](student-guide/)** | The 11-year-old | ~600 lines/week | The chapter. Hook, big idea, worked examples, what we did in class, new words. Read **after** class, or before if they like to be ahead. |
| **[`workbook/`](workbook/)** | The 11-year-old, with a pencil | ~700 lines/week | Warm-up, two practice sets, a puzzle, Think Deeper, Build It pages, Draw It, self-check — and a complete answer key in a collapsible block at the bottom. This is the homework. |

Plus: **[`assessments/`](assessments/)** (four 45-minute, 40-mark term papers with full keys),
**[`projects/`](projects/)** (project ideas, a fully worked example project, the capstone), and
**[`figures/`](figures/)** (451 hand-drawn SVG diagrams, ~12 per week).

> **⚠️ Watch out:** do not hand the student the teacher guide. It contains every answer, and worse,
> it tells them which mistakes they are expected to make.

## 🔁 The weekly rhythm

One class, one homework, every week. That is the whole system.

| When | Who | How long | What |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Night before** | You | 15–20 min | Read the teacher guide's *What YOU Need to Know First* and *Prep Checklist*. Print what it asks for. Do the worked example yourself, cold. |
| **The class** | Both | 60–75 min | Hook (8) → Concept (18) → Worked Example (14) → Activity (20) → Wrap & Assign (10). The teacher guide gives you the clock and the script. |
| **After class** | Student | 20 min | Read the student guide chapter. It re-tells the lesson so nothing rests on memory. |
| **Homework** | Student | 45–60 min | The workbook. They mark it themselves from the answer key when they are done — that is deliberate. |
| **Before next week** | You | 5 min | Skim the workbook's answer key and the teacher guide's *Next Week Preview* / *Prep early* note. |

**Terms.** Nine weeks each. Weeks 9, 18, 27 and 36 are checkpoints, not new material — and each term
ends with a paper from `assessments/`. Weeks 34–36 are the capstone build and showcase.

**If you miss a week, skip a week — do not double up.** The course is cumulative: Week 22 opens an
envelope sealed in Week 19, and Week 33 counts photos taken in Week 15. Order matters more than pace.

## 🪝 The first 30 minutes of Week 1, for a nervous teacher

You are going to feel underprepared. You are not. Here is literally all of it.

**Before they sit down (5 min).** On the table: 12 cut-out cards, the three-bin mat, two copies of
the vending-machine rulebook, a pencil, a notebook, and **a thermostat** — or a photo of one on your
phone, or a microwave. Head a page in the front of the notebook *"Questions We Owe Answers To"*.
Phones on silent, yours included.

**0:00–0:08 — Hook. Tell a story, then ask one question.**
Put the thermostat down and do not explain it. Tell them that in 2016 AlphaGo beat the world's best
Go player and every newspaper printed *machines are thinking now* — and then tell them the bit
nobody printed: **that same program could not play checkers at all.** It had one skill and outside it
was blank. Then pick up the thermostat: *"This decides whether to heat your house. Nobody tells it.
Is this smart?"* Then ask the only question that matters:

> **"Which one is doing something interesting — the Go program, or the thermostat?"**

Any answer with a reason attached is a win. If they shrug: *"Guess anyway, nobody's marking it."*
If they say *the thermostat, because it works with nobody there* — that is the exact misconception
this lesson dismantles. Say *"hold that, I'm coming back to it in twenty minutes,"* and then do.

**0:08–0:26 — Concept. Four words, concrete first, name second.**
The teacher guide gives you the four words in order with a script for each: **artificial
intelligence**, **judgement**, **rule-based system**, **if-then rule**. Draw the thermostat as a
flowchart on the board. The one idea you must land is the test: *could two sensible people disagree
about the answer?* Yes → judgement. No → not judgement, and so not AI.

**0:26–0:30 — Start the worked example.** Put the vending-machine rulebook in front of them and
trace request 1 out loud yourself, finger on the page, saying *"Rule 2 fires → the machine says
SOLD OUT."* Then hand it over.

**That is minute 30.** The rest of the hour is on the clock in
[`teacher-guide/week-01.md`](teacher-guide/week-01.md).

> **💡 Try this:** if you only have 45 minutes, run Hook (8) + Concept (12) + the card sort (15) +
> Wrap (10), and move the vending rulebook to homework. Every week has a short version in its
> §Differentiation.

> **🧑‍🏫 If a student asks something you cannot answer:** write it on the *Questions We Owe Answers
> To* page and say you will find out. That page is part of the method, not an admission of defeat.

## 🖨️ Printing

- **Print the workbook.** It is designed to be written on: blank grids, label-the-diagram figures,
  tally sheets. A4 or US Letter, portrait, single-sided so there is room to work.
- **Before you print a workbook week, collapse the answer key.** It lives in a `<details>` block at
  the bottom. If your viewer prints it open, print pages up to `## ✅ Answers` only.
- **The teacher guide is for screen, not paper.** It is ~1,100 lines a week. Print only the figures
  a week's Prep Checklist explicitly asks for.
- **Figures print in black and white.** The palette was chosen and tested for it — every colour also
  carries a shape, a label or a number, so nothing is lost. Print greyscale and save your colour ink.
- **The one thing worth printing in colour:** Week 24's RGB channel figures, where the colour *is*
  the lesson.
- **No printer at all?** Every activity has a no-printer fallback in its *If something fails* table.
  Week 1's is "write the 12 card names on 12 torn scraps of paper." The course was designed to
  survive this.

## ✅ What you need, and what you do not

**You need:** paper, pencils, scissors, a ruler, a few coloured pens, a browser, and a printer if you
have one. **23 of the 36 weeks need no technology at all.** For the rest:

| Weeks | What you need | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **17, 18, 22, 33, 34, 35** | A laptop with a webcam, and [Teachable Machine](https://teachablemachine.withgoogle.com) in a browser | No account, no install. Week 15 is where you *plan* the photo shoot; Week 17 is the first time you press a button. |
| **6, 10, 12, 26** | A spreadsheet — Google Sheets, Excel Online or LibreOffice Calc | A calculator and paper work for 6, 10 and 12. Week 26 is the one that really wants the spreadsheet. |
| **3** | A browser, for `quickdraw.withgoogle.com` plus **one** chatbot an adult has approved | You drive the chatbot keyboard, not the student. |
| **15** | A phone or tablet camera — for the *homework*, not the class | This is the week you shoot the 120 photos everything later depends on. |

**You do not need:** any coding, anything installed, any account, any paid tool, or any prior AI
knowledge. **There is no programming anywhere in Level 1.** That starts in Level 2.
