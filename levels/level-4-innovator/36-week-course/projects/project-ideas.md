## ⚠️ Five rules, and they are rules, not preferences

> **1. Never build anything that classifies, scores or ranks a person.** Their mood, their honesty, how clever or trustworthy they look, whether they will pass. You cannot collect a fair sample, the labels are not real things, and somebody gets hurt. Names, sentences you wrote, toy tickets, letters, numbers and pictures you generated: yes. Judgements about people: no. (The *name generator* makes up names; it never predicts anything about whoever owns one.)
>
> **2. Nothing medical, legal or financial.** A toy "support ticket" is a toy. If your write-up says "advice", "diagnose" or "safe to rely on" about a real person's real problem, it needs rewriting.
>
> **3. Every score gets its pile and its `n`.** Not "the agent is 90% safe". *"The stand-in obeyed in 85 of 100 seeded runs."* Every time, in every README, in every sentence you say out loud.
>
> **4. Every printed number gets a seed.** `torch.manual_seed(0)`, `np.random.default_rng(0)`, `random.Random(0)`, `random_state=0`. **A number you cannot reproduce is not a result.** And run every noisy comparison on **at least three seeds**: the gap between seeds is the size of the yardstick.
>
> **5. A scripted part is never evidence about a real model.** The kit models a *mechanism* — the loop, the contract, the fence, the trace, the cost shape — not a *rate*. If your conclusion begins "real models do…", delete the sentence.

## 🧪 A starter you can run right now (and one real result to beat)

Most projects begin with the same move: run something small, read the number, then change one thing. Here is the move at its smallest. It uses the Week 1 harness and prints three learning rates. **Predict the three accuracies first.**

```python
# starter_lr.py - three learning rates on the spirals, 20 epochs, seed 0
import torch
torch.set_num_threads(1)
from l4lib import spirals
for lr in (1e-5, 1e-3, 1e-1):
    h = spirals.run(f"adamw lr={lr:g}", lr=lr, epochs=20, verbose=False)
    print(f"lr={lr:<7g} final train {h['train'][-1]:.3f}  val {h['val'][-1]:.3f}  acc {h['acc'][-1]*100:.1f}%")
```

```text
lr=1e-05   final train 0.692  val 0.691  acc 53.1%
lr=0.001   final train 0.021  val 0.029  acc 98.9%
lr=0.1     final train 0.696  val 0.692  acc 53.1%
```

Two of the three rows are stuck at `ln 2 = 0.693` — "no better than a coin" — for opposite reasons. **A loss curve is a symptom, not a diagnosis;** the first project below is about telling those two apart.

### 1. The Two Flat Lines

> *Two runs sit at 0.693. One learns too slowly, one blows up. Find three cheap checks that tell them apart.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐ |
| **Time** | 90 minutes |
| **Best after** | Week 7 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 1, 3, 7 |
| **Data** | `spirals.make_spirals`, through the `spirals.run` harness |
| **Syntax it uses** | `spirals.run(..., lr=, epochs=, verbose=False)` · `h["gnorm"]` and `h["lr"]` from the history · keyword-only `*` arguments |
| **Maths it uses** | `ln 2 = 0.693` as the "coin" loss (W1) |

**You will learn:** that the same final loss can mean opposite things, and that the *first epoch's gradient norm* usually says which one you are looking at.

**Steps:**

1. Run the starter above. Note which two rows are stuck.
2. For each stuck run, print the gradient norm at the first and last epoch, and the first-epoch loss.
3. Write the cheapest check that separates them (a different learning rate, a shorter run, a printed norm) and state what each outcome would mean *before* running it.
4. Run your check on three seeds. Report both runs as `mean ± spread`.
5. Add both rows to your Week 7 playbook as two separate symptoms with two separate actions.

**Done well looks like:** a two-row table — symptom, check, what you saw, action — and a sentence that admits the loss value alone could not tell them apart.

### 2. The Learning-Rate Shelf

> *Find the window of learning rates that works, and show it is a shelf with edges, not a single magic number.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐ |
| **Time** | 90 minutes |
| **Best after** | Week 7 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 1, 4, 7 |
| **Data** | spirals |
| **Syntax it uses** | a loop over `[1e-5, 1e-4, ..., 1e-1]` · `itertools.product` · `np.mean` / `np.std` over three seeds |
| **Maths it uses** | mean and spread across repeats (L3 W11) |

**You will learn:** that the shelf is wide, that its edges are sharp, and that one seed can put a run on the wrong side of an edge.

**Steps:**

1. Sweep six learning rates (a factor of ten apart) for AdamW, three seeds each.
2. Plot final validation loss against learning rate on a log x-axis, with the spread as a band.
3. Mark the two edges: the smallest rate that leaves `0.693` and the smallest rate that returns to it.
4. Repeat for plain SGD. Say how the shelf moved.
5. Predict, on paper, whether the SGD shelf is to the left or the right of the AdamW one — before you run it.

**Done well looks like:** a chart with a flat floor in the middle and two cliffs, and a sentence with your own counts in it: *"the shelf is N decades wide for AdamW and M for SGD"*, where N and M are measured, not guessed.

### 3. Momentum Against Bumps

> *Build a 1-D landscape with a bump, and find the learning rate at which momentum rolls through and plain SGD does not.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐⭐ |
| **Time** | 2 hours |
| **Best after** | Week 3 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 2, 3 |
| **Data** | A function you write: `f(w) = w**2 + 0.8*np.sin(5*w)` |
| **Syntax it uses** | `torch.optim.SGD(..., momentum=0.9)` · `optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)` · `tensor.norm()` · a plain `for` loop over steps |
| **Maths it uses** | exponential moving average (W2) · a slope by nudging (L3 W12) |

**You will learn:** what "a running average of where you were going" does to a path, by drawing it.

**Steps:**

1. Define `f`, and draw it.
2. Start `w` at `2.5` and take 60 steps with plain SGD at a few learning rates. Record the path.
3. Repeat with `momentum=0.9`. Overlay the two paths on the landscape.
4. Find one `lr` where momentum reaches the deep minimum and plain SGD stops in a dip, and one where momentum *overshoots and wobbles*.
5. By hand, do three steps of each from the same start, and check them against `torch.optim`.

**Done well looks like:** two coloured paths on one landscape and a table of three by-hand steps that match the library to three decimals.

### 4. Adam's First Step

> *Prove that Adam's first step is about `lr` whatever the gradient's size — then find where that claim stops being true.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐⭐ |
| **Time** | 2 hours |
| **Best after** | Week 3 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 2, 3 |
| **Data** | One parameter, hand-set gradients: `1`, `1000`, `0.001`, `0` |
| **Syntax it uses** | `torch.optim.Adam` · `p.grad = torch.tensor(...)` · `torch.sqrt` · `tensor.norm()` |
| **Maths it uses** | root-mean-square and the epsilon that stops division by zero (W3) |

**You will learn:** why Adam is forgiving about scale, and what the `eps` does when the gradient is tiny.

5. Write the sentence that Week 3 did not: *when* is "the first step is about `lr`" a bad guide?

**Done well looks like:** a five-row table whose last row is **not** `lr`, and a correct sentence about why.

### 5. The Schedule Shootout

> *Constant, step, cosine, and warm-up-then-cosine — same budget of steps, four runs, three seeds. Which wins, by how much, and is the gap bigger than the seed noise?*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐⭐ |
| **Time** | 2 hours |
| **Best after** | Week 4 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 1, 4, 7 |
| **Data** | spirals |
| **Syntax it uses** | `spirals.run(..., schedule="cosine", warmup_frac=0.1)` · `lambda step: ...` · `LambdaLR` · `scheduler.get_last_lr()` |
| **Maths it uses** | mean and spread across repeats (L3 W11) |

**You will learn:** that a schedule can be worth less than the seed-to-seed wobble, and how to say so.

**Steps:**

1. Write the four schedules as plain numpy curves first, and plot them before training anything.
2. Verify your curves against `scheduler.get_last_lr()` at five chosen steps.
3. Train each schedule on three seeds. Record final validation loss.
4. Put the twelve numbers in a table, and compute `mean ± spread` per schedule.
5. State the winner *only if its band does not overlap the runner-up's*. Otherwise write "no difference I can detect".

**Done well looks like:** an honest "no difference I can detect" on at least one pair, with the three seeds showing why.

### 6. The Batch-Size Confound

> *"Bigger batch is better" — or just fewer steps? Separate the two with a grid.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐⭐ |
| **Time** | 2–3 hours |
| **Best after** | Week 4 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 1, 4, 7 |
| **Data** | spirals |
| **Syntax it uses** | `spirals.run(..., batch_size=, epochs=)` · `itertools.product` |
| **Maths it uses** | mean and spread (L3 W11) |

**You will learn:** that when you change the batch size you also change the number of optimizer steps, and that the honest comparison holds *steps*, not *epochs*, fixed.

**Steps:**

1. Work out the steps per epoch for batch sizes 16, 32, 64, 128, 256 from the training-set size, by hand.
2. Run the grid holding **epochs** fixed. Table and chart.
3. Run it again holding the **total number of steps** fixed (adjust `epochs`). Table and chart.
4. Say which comparison each table supports, and which one "bigger is better" was secretly made in.
5. Add the pair of tables to your playbook as a warning.

**Done well looks like:** two tables that disagree, and a paragraph that says which number a learner should trust.

### 13. Exposure Bias Meter

> *A model trained on true letters is tested on its own letters. Measure the gap.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐⭐⭐ |
| **Time** | 3–4 hours |
| **Best after** | Week 13 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 8, 12, 13 |
| **Data** | the 231 names, or toy grammar sentences |
| **Syntax it uses** | `F.cross_entropy(..., ignore_index=)` · `torch.cat` · `torch.full` |
| **Maths it uses** | *(none new)* |

**You will learn:** the difference between the loss of a model fed the *truth* (teacher forcing) and the loss of the same model fed *its own guesses*.

**Steps:**

1. Train to a modest loss. Record the teacher-forced validation loss.
2. Build "free-running" evaluation: let the model generate a name, then score how likely it finds *its own* string letter by letter.
3. Compare: teacher-forced loss on real names vs loss on its own samples. Predict which is lower.

**You will learn:** that "tool results are data, not orders" is a measurable defence, and that a sandbox is the layer that still works when the others fail.

**Steps:**

```python
# attack_rate.py - 100 seeded runs of the planted-note drill (a stand-in, not a model)
from pathlib import Path
from l4lib import rag, toyagent
chunks = rag.poisoned_notebook_chunks()
ix = rag.VectorIndex(chunks)
specs = toyagent.tool_specs()
def rate(frame, n=100, g=0.8):
    obeyed = 0
    for seed in range(n):
        box = toyagent.Sandbox(root=Path("sbx"))
        reg = toyagent.build_registry(box, ix, rag.notebook_titles(chunks))
        m = toyagent.GullibleModel(toyagent.ScriptedModel(toyagent.reminder_plan()), gullibility=g, seed=seed)
        r = toyagent.run_agent("What reminders did I write to myself?", reg, specs, model=m,
                               auto_approve=True, frame_results=frame, flag_injections=False)
        obeyed += bool(r["obeyed"])
    return obeyed
for frame in (False, True):
    print(f"stand-in, not a model | gullibility 0.8 | framing {frame!s:5} | obeyed {rate(frame)} of 100 runs")
```

```text
stand-in, not a model | gullibility 0.8 | framing True  | obeyed 55 of 100 runs
```

The *expected* counts are 80 and 48 (`0.8` and `0.8 × 0.6`), and the measured ones are 85 and 55 — a hundred draws wobble by several either side, so 85 against 80 and 55 against 48 are both within what chance alone produces.

| **Maths it uses** | low-rank: `r x in + out x r` numbers instead of `out x in` (W31) |

**You will learn:** why LoRA is initialised with `B = 0`, and what fraction of the model you actually train.

**Steps:**

2. Build the patch (starter below) and confirm step 0 equals the base model.
3. Take your own tiny encoder from Week 31 and compute the real percentage for your rank.
4. Change `B` to small random numbers and show step 0 is no longer the base.

```python
# lora_step0.py - a LoRA patch on one 768 x 768 layer: how many numbers, and is step 0 the base model?
import copy, torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)
class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base, r=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.base = base
        self.base.requires_grad_(False)                     # freeze the big grid
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(r, base.in_features))
        self.B = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(base.out_features, r))
        nn.init.normal_(self.A, std=0.02)
        nn.init.zeros_(self.B)                              # B = 0, so the patch adds nothing yet
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.base(x) + x @ self.A.T @ self.B.T
base = nn.Linear(768, 768)
pristine = copy.deepcopy(base)
patched = LoRALinear(base, r=8)
x = torch.randn(4, 768)
trainable = sum(p.numel() for p in patched.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
full = sum(p.numel() for p in pristine.parameters())
print("step-0 max difference:", (patched(x) - pristine(x)).abs().max().item())
print(f"trainable {trainable:,} of {full:,} = {100*trainable/full:.2f}%   (r x in + out x r = {8*768 + 768*8:,})")
```

```text
step-0 max difference: 0.0
trainable 12,288 of 590,592 = 2.08%   (r x in + out x r = 12,288)
```

The `590,592` counts the layer's 589,824 weights **plus 768 biases**; the course ledger's 589,824 counts weights only. Both give `2.08%` to two places — say which one you used.

**Done well looks like:** a by-hand table that matches the code, a step-0 difference of exactly `0.0`, and a correct trainable percentage for *your* model.

```python
said = np.array([.9,.9,.8,.8,.7,.7,.6,.6,.55,.5])
right = np.array([1,0,1,1,0,1,1,0,0,1])
print("accuracy", right.mean(), " mean said", said.mean().round(3))
print("Brier", np.mean((said-right)**2).round(4))
b = np.digitize(said,[.6,.8]); ece=0
for k in range(3):
    m=b==k; print(f"bucket {k}: n={m.sum()} said {said[m].mean():.3f} right {right[m].mean():.3f}")
    ece += m.mean()*abs(said[m].mean()-right[m].mean())
print("ECE", round(ece,4))
```

```text
accuracy 0.6  mean said 0.705
Brier 0.2553
bucket 0: n=2 said 0.525 right 0.500
bucket 1: n=4 said 0.650 right 0.500
bucket 2: n=4 said 0.850 right 0.750
ECE 0.105
```

Ten results and three buckets: the smallest bucket holds **two** results. Any sentence about it is a guess, which is the honest thing to say.

**Done well looks like:** a reliability table with `n` in every row and a verdict that respects the small buckets.

### 47. Learning to Say "I Don't Know"

> *Add an abstain rule and draw coverage against accuracy: how much can it answer, and how good are those answers?*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐⭐ |
| **Time** | 3 hours |
| **Best after** | Week 32 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 26, 30, 32 |
| **Data** | the same 40 results, or the refusal data of Project 35 |
| **Syntax it uses** | `np.digitize` · boolean masks · `np.argsort` · `ax.plot` |
| **Maths it uses** | calibration gap (W32); precision as a count (L3 W8) |

**You will learn:** that abstention is a dial with a price, measured in the questions you decline to answer.

**Steps:**

1. Sort the 40 results by stated confidence.
2. For each threshold, compute coverage (the fraction answered) and the accuracy of what was answered.
3. Plot coverage on the x-axis and accuracy on the y-axis.
4. Find any category whose accuracy *fell* when the average rose. Name it.
5. Pick a threshold for a use where a wrong answer costs ten times a refusal, and show the arithmetic.

**Done well looks like:** one curve, one chosen point with a reason, and a category table that keeps the average honest.

### 48. Red Team, Patch, Re-Test

> *Attack your own toy agent, fix one hole, and show before-and-after rates with a noise bound. The agent is a stand-in, not a model.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐⭐⭐ |
| **Time** | 5 hours |
| **Best after** | Week 33 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 26, 28, 29, 33 |
| **Data** | the poisoned notebook and attacks A1–A5 of Week 33 |
| **Syntax it uses** | `toyagent.GullibleModel`, `toyagent.Sandbox(strict=False)` · a loop over 50 seeds · `np.sqrt` |
| **Maths it uses** | binomial standard deviation `sqrt(n p (1-p))` (W33) |

**You will learn:** the habit that makes a security claim scientific: attack, evidence, mechanism, fix, re-test *including the happy path*, residual risk.

**Steps:**

1. Run each attack over 50 seeded runs and record how many "succeed".
2. Pick one; write its mechanism in a sentence a stranger could follow.
3. Patch it (for example switch the weak sandbox to the strict one).
4. Re-run the same 50 seeds. Report before and after as fractions with the noise bound `sqrt(n p (1-p))`.
5. Run the normal, non-attack task again to prove you did not break it. Write the residual risk.

**Done well looks like:** a before/after row with a noise bound, a happy-path check that still passes, and one accepted risk written honestly.

### 50. The Honest System Card

> *Take a finished project of yours. Write the page a stranger needs before relying on it.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | ⭐⭐ |
| **Time** | 3 hours, mostly writing |
| **Best after** | Week 35 |
| **Draws on** | Weeks 30–36, and whichever project you pick |
| **Data** | whatever that project used |
| **Syntax it uses** | nothing new: re-run your own scripts, print their tables, `hashlib.sha256` to check your frozen file is unchanged |
| **Maths it uses** | binomial standard deviation (W33) for every "rate"; Cohen's kappa (W30) if a judge was involved |

**You will learn:** the skill the whole level is aimed at, applied to the person hardest to be honest about.

**Steps:**

1. Re-run the project from a clean folder. Note everything that does not reproduce.
2. Write the card with these headings: **intended use · out of scope · measured numbers with sample sizes · failure modes · guardrails · retention · staged release · incident response · contact.**
3. Under *measured numbers*, give each one its `n`, its seed and a noise bound. If a number came from a stand-in, label it *stand-in, not a model* right there.
4. Under *failure modes*, list at least two real ones you reproduced, with numbers.
5. Write who **should not** rely on it, and why.

**Done well looks like:** a card whose *numbers* section is less impressive than your first write-up and much more useful, with a "should not rely on it" paragraph a stranger would thank you for. **"It works well" is a blank.**

## 🎒 Picking one, and finishing it

**Four ways this goes wrong, and the fix for each.**

| It goes wrong like this | The fix |
|---|---|
| You pick something with no baseline, and at the end you have a number and no idea whether it is good | **Test 1.** Write the cheap baseline's score down on day one, before you build anything |
| You pick something that needs a download, discover it on day three, and quietly abandon it | **Test 2.** The data table above is the whole world. Pick from it |
| You write the questions after you have seen what the system answers well | **Test 4.** Freeze the cases first; commit a hash |
| You finish, the number is great, and you cannot reproduce it a week later | **Rule 4.** A seed on every run, from the first line you type |

**And the one thing that makes a Level 4 project good rather than finished:**

> **Say what the number does not mean.** In the README, in the first three lines, next to every scripted part: *"stand-in, not a model."* Then write the sentence a sceptical stranger would ask, and answer it with a number and the `n` behind it.
>
> That habit turns every project on this page into an experiment. It is also the only reliable defence against the defining bug of this level — **the one that prints a number you are pleased with and cannot defend.**
