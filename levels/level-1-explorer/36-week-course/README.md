# 🧭 Level 1 Explorer — The 36-Week Course

### *One school year. One class a week. A real trained model, honestly measured, by a Grade 6 student.*

**Learner:** one 6th grader, age ~11 · **Teacher:** any adult, no AI knowledge required
**Coding:** none · **Maths:** fractions, percentages, reading a table
**Rhythm:** 36 weeks × one 60–75 minute class + 45–60 min of homework

[⬅ Back to Level 1](../README.md) · [**How to use this course (4 min read)**](HOW-TO-USE.md) · [Start here: Teacher Orientation](teacher-guide/00-orientation.md) · [Figure style guide](figures/STYLE.md)

---

## 🪝 What This Is

The nine Level 1 modules next door are excellent and they are **not a course**. They are a book. A
book assumes a motivated reader with three free hours and nobody waiting on them.

This folder turns that book into a **school year**. Same ideas, same honesty, same refusal to say
"magic" — but sliced into 36 sittings, each one small enough to actually happen on a Tuesday evening
when everyone is tired.

The single design constraint that shaped every page:

> **The teacher opens the week's file 20 minutes before class, and that is all the preparation
> they get.** No outside reading. No prior AI knowledge. No degree. They must be able to teach a
> confident, correct, 70-minute lesson from that one file.

Everything else follows from that. Every lesson has its own script. Every question a student can
ask has a written answer. Every number in every example is worked out in full. Nothing says
"see the module for details".

### What the student walks out with in June

- A **trained image classifier** they built themselves, with a real accuracy number they computed
  **by hand** on photos the model had never seen
- A **hand-drawn confusion matrix** of their own model's mistakes
- A **measured bias report** naming one group of inputs their model fails on, with the gap in
  percentage points and the arithmetic for the fix
- A **working Scratch app** that reacts to the model's predictions
- A **five-minute live demo** delivered to a real adult, with the word "magic" banned
- And the reflex that matters most: hearing "95% accurate" and immediately asking
  *"out of how many, and what's the baseline?"*

|---|---|
| **The learner** | One 6th grader, around 11 years old. Reads comfortably. Can do fractions and percentages. Has never written a line of code and will not write one this year. Curious, easily bored, allergic to being talked down to. |
| **The teacher** | You. A parent, a general classroom teacher, a librarian, a volunteer, an older sibling. **You are not expected to know anything about AI.** Read [`teacher-guide/00-orientation.md`](teacher-guide/00-orientation.md) once — it is a complete crash course — and you are ahead of most adults. |
| **The setting** | A kitchen table, a classroom, a library corner. One laptop with a webcam. A browser. That is the entire technical requirement. |

> **💡 Try this:** If you are teaching this to your own child, do the activities *with* them rather

**The rules:**

1. **The student never opens the teacher guide.** It contains the answers and the "what they'll get
   wrong" notes. Reading it spoils the discovery that the lesson is built around.
2. **The teacher does not skip the teacher guide** even on the weeks that look easy. The first
   section of every teacher file is *"What you need to understand before you teach this"* — that is
   where your own confidence comes from.
3. **The workbook answer key is at the bottom of the workbook, not hidden in another file.**
   The student is trusted with it and told, in writing, to struggle for fifteen minutes first.
   Fifteen minutes of struggle is the lesson; the answer is just the receipt.

## 📋 All 36 Weeks

**Type key:** 🟦 teach · 🟩 lab · 🟨 project · 🟪 review · 🟥 assessment · 🎪 capstone

| Week | Term | Title | Big idea | Type | Homework |
|:--:|:--:|---|---|:--:|---|
| 1 | 1 | Is It Smart, or Is It Just Following Orders? | AI is a machine doing a job that used to need a person's judgement — not a brain, not magic. | 🟦 teach | AI Spotter's Log part 1 — 8 systems you touched in 24 hours, each with one sentence on who wrote the rules |
| 2 | 1 | The Machine That Learns the Rule By Itself | In machine learning nobody writes the rule; the machine finds it by studying labelled examples. | 🟦 teach | Rewrite five "magic" sentences about AI into honest ones, and write the one-sentence definition from memory |
| 3 | 1 | AI Detective: Find 15 in Your Own Day | Almost every AI you meet is narrow — brilliant at one job, blank at everything else. | 🟩 lab | Finish the AI Spotter's Log: 15 rows sorted into rules / learned / generating, plus 3 hard calls defended in writing |
| 4 | 1 | Everything a Machine Knows Arrived as a Table | Whatever an AI knows arrived as rows and columns — one row is one thing, one column is one measurement. | 🟦 teach | Start "Your Life In 30 Rows": choose the row unit, write 4 column headers, collect the first 7 rows |
| 5 | 1 | Numbers, Names, and Broken Boxes | Every column has a type, and the four kinds of mess — missing, duplicate, impossible, typo — are findable if you look. | 🟦 teach | Run all four checks on your own 30-row table; log every fault you find and the fix you made |
| 6 | 1 | Where Did This Data Come From? | Thirty rows about you are a sample, not the world — and a data card is how you stay honest about that. | 🟩 lab | Finish "Your Life In 30 Rows" plus the 7-line data card, and write 3 sentences your data does NOT prove |
| 7 | 1 | Patterns: Things That Repeat Enough to Bet On | A pattern repeats often enough that betting on it beats guessing; a rule is a pattern written down for a computer. | 🟦 teach | Find one real pattern in your own week-6 table by counting, and write it as an if-then rule with a threshold |
| 8 | 1 | Where Rules Break: Edge Cases and False Alarms | Every rule has edge cases, and the two ways to be wrong — false alarms and misses — hurt different people. | 🟦 teach | Write the 138cm-at-a-140cm-ride edge case both ways, then sharpen five vague rules into testable conditions |
| 9 | 1 | Term 1 Checkpoint — Rules on Trial | A rulebook scored on the messages you wrote it from tells you nothing; the honest score is on messages it has never seen. | 🟪 review | Term 1 reflection sheet; fix your rulebook, re-score on 5 more fresh messages, and say which fix broke something else |
| 10 | 2 | The Rule Explosion: Why Nobody Writes 258 Rules | Adding rules to cover exceptions grows faster than the exceptions do — that wall is why machine learning was invented. | 🟨 project | Finish "Rulebook vs Reality": 5-rule spam detector, scored on 10 fresh messages, plus a page on why you stopped adding rules |
| 11 | 2 | How a Machine Describes Your Dog | A feature is one measured description of one example; the label is the answer you want back. | 🟦 teach | Measure 5 kitchen objects into a 5-column feature table, with a written measuring instruction for every column |
| 12 | 2 | Useful, Useless, and Sneaky Features | A feature is only useful if it beats guessing — and a leaky feature already contains the answer. | 🟦 teach | The leak hunt: 6 scenarios, mark the leaky feature and say why it won't exist at prediction time; score your own features against baseline |
| 13 | 2 | Which One? or How Much? | Classification picks from a short list; regression predicts a number — the same table does either, depending on which column you cover. | 🟦 teach | Flip all ten tasks from classification to regression and back; bucket one number label into three named ranges |
| 14 | 2 | Feature Card Deck: Make a Human Be the Model | If your features are good enough, a stranger who has never seen the thing can guess its label from the numbers alone. | 🟩 lab | Finish the 20-card deck, test it on a second human, and write which feature the testers actually used and how you know |
| 15 | 2 | Training: Turning 120 Photos Into a Guessing Machine | Training reads labelled examples once and produces a model; after that the examples are gone and only the model remains. | 🟦 teach | Take and sort 40 photos each of 3 objects following the variety checklist, and log the counts per background and lighting |
| 16 | 2 | Confidence Is Not Correctness | A confidence score is guess strength, not a promise, and the margin tells you how close the race was. | 🟦 teach | Read eight confidence readouts and compute every margin; then write your own confidence policy with a threshold you can justify |
| 17 | 2 | Train Your First Real Model | You can build a working image classifier in twenty minutes without code — and it will be exactly as good as the photos you gave it. | 🟩 lab | Re-test your model on 5 objects it has never seen, log every prediction, and explain the one that surprised you most |
| 18 | 2 | Term 2 Checkpoint — Break Your Own Model on Purpose | Change one thing at a time and the model will tell you exactly what it was relying on. | 🟪 review | Complete the 5-row controlled-experiment table with a written explanation of every row; Term 2 reflection sheet |
| 19 | 3 | The Test You Can't Study For | Testing a model on the examples it trained on is cheating; you must hide examples before training and never let it see them. | 🟦 teach | Take 25 photos per class across two different days, seal day 2 in a signed envelope, and do not open it |
| 20 | 3 | Accuracy, Three Ways — and the Number That Lies | Accuracy is correct ÷ total, and one accuracy number can hide a class that is completely broken. | 🟦 teach | Five accuracy problems shown as fraction, decimal and percentage with the division written out; then find the body the average was hiding |
| 21 | 3 | Memorizing vs Generalizing | A model scoring 100% on its own photos and 40% on new ones didn't learn the object — it learned the photos. | 🟦 teach | Score somebody else's 15-photo test sheet and draw its confusion matrix, then name what got confused with what |
| 22 | 3 | The Hidden Ten: Test Your Own Model Honestly | The only number worth reporting is the one you got on examples the model had never seen. | 🟨 project | Finish "The Hidden Ten": scoring sheet, accuracy three ways, hand-drawn confusion matrix, and a 3-sentence verdict naming your worst class |
| 23 | 3 | A Photo Is Just a Grid of Numbers | To a computer a picture is nothing but a grid of brightness numbers from 0 to 255. | 🟦 teach | Convert a second 12×12 drawing to numbers, then answer six questions about a number grid without ever seeing the picture |
| 24 | 3 | Colour Is Three Grids Stacked | A colour picture is three number grids — red, green, blue — and shrinking an image throws information away forever. | 🟦 teach | Colour by numbers both directions; then shrink a 12×12 grid to 6×6 to 3×3 and list exactly what was lost |
| 25 | 3 | Filters: The Little Grid That Finds Edges | Slide a tiny grid of numbers over an image and it highlights one thing; an edge is a sudden change in brightness. | 🟦 teach | Compute six cells of the horizontal filter by hand, combine with the vertical result, and shade the combined edge map |
| 26 | 3 | Pixel Lab: Make the Outline Appear | Edges survive a change of lighting and raw brightness does not — that is why vision models look for edges first. | 🟩 lab | Finish Pixel Lab: two shaded 12×12 grids and one cell's arithmetic in full; prove edges beat brightness under two lamps |
| 27 | 3 | Term 3 Checkpoint — From Pixels to Words | Everything a machine handles first becomes numbers in a table; text is no different from a photo. | 🟪 review | Term 3 reflection sheet; tokenize a 60-word paragraph from a real book and count its word frequencies |
| 28 | 4 | Counting Word Pairs: The Whole Engine | A language model is a giant tally of which word tends to follow which — nothing more mysterious than that. | 🟦 teach | Build a next-word table from a 60-word corpus you choose yourself, then read four questions off it |
| 29 | 4 | Dice, Fluency, and Making Things Up | Randomness is why the same prompt gives different answers — and a sentence can be perfectly fluent and completely false. | 🟦 teach | Generate three sentences with fixed dice rolls and one greedily; find the hallucination and explain where the falseness came from |
| 30 | 4 | Build a Chatbot in Scratch | A rule-based bot and a learned language model fail in completely different ways, and knowing which you're talking to changes your trust. | 🟩 lab | Finish "The Human Language Model": tally sheet, 3 generated sentences, running Scratch bot, and 3 sentences comparing how each fails |
| 31 | 4 | Who Is Missing From the Photos? | Bias is not the machine being mean — it is a gap in who was in the training data, showing up as a gap in accuracy. | 🟦 teach | Compute a fairness gap from a given 48-photo audit; then predict which group YOUR model will fail on, in writing, before testing |
| 32 | 4 | Your Data, Fakes, and Trusting Too Much | A photo carries more than a picture, a confident answer is not a correct one, and something real-looking can be made in ten minutes. | 🟦 teach | Audit a real product's data map with five questions; then fact-check three confident AI answers against a real source |
| 33 | 4 | Audit Your Own Model | The most trustworthy thing you can do with your own model is show, with numbers, the group it fails on. | 🟩 lab | Finish the Fairness Audit Poster: data map, measured gap in percentage points, the fix priced in photos, and a warning sign for a real user |
| 34 | 4 | The AI Fair Booth, Part 1: Build It and Test It Honestly | A real AI project is not the model — it is the model plus the honest paperwork saying what it can and cannot do. | 🎪 capstone | Capstone milestones 1–4 evidence: brief, data card draft, saved model file, sealed-envelope scoring sheet with accuracy and matrix |
| 35 | 4 | The AI Fair Booth, Part 2: The App, the Report, the Demo | If you can show your model failing and explain why, you understand it — anyone can show it working. | 🎪 capstone | Rehearse the 5-minute demo three times, twice to a real human; prepare answers to all six question-bank questions; print the booth signs |
| 36 | 4 | Showcase Day: Prove It, Present It, Celebrate It | You can build a guessing machine, measure it honestly, find who it fails, and explain all of it without saying "magic". | 🟥 assessment | No new homework — complete the Level 2 gate self-check and write a letter to yourself about what you want to build next |

## 🛟 If Something Goes Wrong

| Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|
| "Train Model" is greyed out | A class has zero samples. Every class needs at least one; aim for 30+. Delete empty classes with ⋮ → Delete Class. |
| Webcam is a black rectangle | Another app has the camera (Zoom, Teams, FaceTime, a second tab). Quit them all, reload. Then click the 🔒 padlock in the address bar → Camera → Allow. |
| The tab freezes or crashes while training | Training runs on your laptop, not in the cloud. Close other tabs. Keep classes to 30–120 samples. Use Chrome rather than Safari. |
| Every class sits near 33% no matter what | The classes look the same *to the model* — same background, same light, same distance. Re-collect with the Week 15 variety checklist. |
| The model vanished when the tab closed | There is no autosave. Always ☰ → **Download project as file** before closing. Name it `baseline-v1.tm`, not `Untitled.tm`. |
| A lesson ran out of time | Cut the ✍️ practice, never the 🎲 activity. Practice moves to homework; the activity is where the learning lives. |
| The student is bored | You are explaining for too long. The teacher guide caps every explanation at 12 minutes for a reason. Move to the activity early. |
| **You** don't understand something | Open [`teacher-guide/00-orientation.md`](teacher-guide/00-orientation.md) and search it. If it isn't there, say "I don't know, let's find out" out loud — Section 8 of the orientation shows you how to do that well. |

## 🧾 The Promise

Thirty-six weeks from now, someone will ask your student what AI is.

They will not say "robots". They will not say "a computer brain". They will not say "magic".

They will say something like: *"It's a system that turns examples into a guessing machine. I built
one that tells my three toothbrushes apart. It's 87% accurate on photos it had never seen — I worked
that out by hand, 13 out of 15. It's worse in lamplight, because 94% of my training photos were taken
in daylight, and I can show you the gap: 50 percentage points. Want to see it fail?"*

And then they will show you, because showing your model failing is the most honest thing an engineer
can do.
